# Debt Instruments

A debt instrument is a paper or electronic obligation that enables the issuing party (the advertiser) to raise funds by promising to repay a lender in accordance with terms of a contract. In simple terms, it is a "I Owe You" (IOU) that can be traded between investors.

## Core Concepts

Unlike Equities, which represent ownership in a company, Debt Instruments represent a **creditor relationship**. The investor lends money to an issuer (such as a corporation or government) for a defined period.

### Key Characteristics

1.  **Principal (Par Value)**: The amount of money the issuer borrows and must repay at the end of the term.
2.  **Interest (Coupon)**: The "rent" paid by the issuer for using the investor's money. This can be fixed, floating, or zero (zero-coupon bonds).
3.  **Maturity**: The specific date on which the principal amount of the debt is due to be paid.
4.  **Seniority**: In the event of bankruptcy, debt holders are typically paid before equity holders.

## Classification of Debt

The debt instrument domain is categorized based on the duration of the loan and the complexity of the payout structure:

*   **[Money Market Instruments](money_market/CONTEXT.md)**: Short-term debt obligations with a maturity of less than one year. These are used for high liquidity and low-risk "cash equivalent" positions.
*   **[Notes](Note/CONTEXT.md)**: Medium-term debt obligations, often issued under programs (like MTNs) that allow for flexibility in timing and structure.
*   **[Bonds](Bond/CONTEXT.md)**: Long-term debt instruments used by governments and large corporations to fund major projects or operations.

## Why Use Debt Instruments?

Investors choose debt instruments primarily for **Capital Preservation** and **Income Generation**. While they generally offer lower potential returns than stocks, they provide a more predictable stream of cash flows and higher security in the capital structure.
