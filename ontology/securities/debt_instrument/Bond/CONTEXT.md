# Bond in the Context of Securities

When you buy a bond, you aren't buying a piece of a company (that’s a stock); instead, you are loaning money to an entity—usually a government or a corporation. In exchange for your cash today, they promise to pay you back the full amount later, plus regular interest payments along the way.

## Common Types of Bonds

| Type              | Issuer              | Risk Level                                                     |
|-------------------|---------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Treasuries        | Federal Government   | Lowest (backed by the government's ability to tax).            |
| Municipal Bonds   | Cities/States        | Low (often come with tax advantages).                          |
| Corporate Bonds   | Private Companies    | Varies (depends on the company's financial health).            |
| High-Yield (Junk) | Struggling Companies | High (higher interest, but higher risk of default).            |

## How a Bond Works

To understand a bond, you need to know three specific terms:

- **Face Value (Par Value)**: The amount of money the bondholder will get back once the bond matures. If you buy a \$1,000 bond, that \$1,000 is the face value.

- **Coupon Rate**: The interest rate the issuer pays the bondholder. If a \$1,000 bond has a 5% coupon, you’ll receive \$50 a year until it expires.

- **Maturity Date**: The "deadline" when the loan ends and the issuer must pay back the original face value.

## Why Do People Buy Bonds?

Bonds are generally seen as "safer" than stocks, though they usually offer lower potential returns. Investors use them for:

- **Income**: The regular interest payments provide a steady stream of cash.

- **Safety**: If a company goes bust, bondholders are paid before stockholders.

- **Diversification**: When the stock market gets rocky, bonds often stay stable or even rise in value.

## The Inverse Relationship: Price vs. Yield

This is the part that trips most people up: When interest rates go up, bond prices go down. Imagine you have a bond paying 3%. If the central bank raises interest rates and new bonds start paying 5%, nobody wants your 3% bond anymore. To sell it, you’d have to lower your price.

The Rule of Thumb:  **Interest Rates ↑ = Bond Prices ↓** or **Interest Rates ↓ = Bond Prices ↑**

## What is the difference between yield and coupon?

### The Coupon (The Fixed Promise)

The coupon is the fixed annual interest rate established when the bond is first issued. It is calculated as a percentage of the face value (usually $1,000).

- It stays the same: Unless it's a "floating rate" bond, the dollar amount of the coupon payment never changes throughout the life of the bond.

- Calculation: If you have a \$1,000 bond with a 5% coupon, you will receive \$50 every year, regardless of what happens to the economy.

### The Yield (The Reality Check)

**The yield** is the **actual return** you get on your investment. This number moves up and down because bond prices fluctuate on the open market.

- Current Yield: This is calculated by taking the annual coupon payment and dividing it by the bond's current market price.

- Why it changes: If you bought that same \$1,000 bond for a discount (say, \$900) because interest rates rose, your "yield" is actually higher than 5% because you're getting that same $50 payment on a smaller initial investment.

#### Comparison at a Glance

| Feature     | Coupon Rate                              | Current Yield                                      |
|------------|-------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Definition | Interest rate based on Face Value.        | Return based on Market Price.                      |
| Stability  | Fixed for the life of the bond.           | Changes daily as bond prices move.                 |
| Formula    | $(\text{Annual Coupon Payment} / \text{Face Value})$ | $(\text{Annual Coupon Payment} / \text{Current Market Price})$ |
| Focus      | What the issuer pays.                     | What the investor earns.                           |

#### Why does this matter?

If you hold a bond until it matures, the yield doesn't matter as much to you—you’ll get your interest and your $1,000 back.

However, if you want to sell your bond before it expires, the yield is everything. If market interest rates have gone up to 7%, nobody will buy your 5% coupon bond unless you drop the price so that its yield matches the current market rate of 7%.

The "Teeter-Totter" Rule: When the Price of a bond goes up, the Yield goes down. When the Price goes down, the Yield goes up.

## Buying scenarios for Bonds

#### Understanding Bond Yields vs. Prices

Imagine a company issues a **\$1,000 bond** with a **5% coupon**. This means the bondholder receives **\$50** every year.

## Scenario A: Buying at "Par" (Face Value)
If you buy the bond for exactly $1,000, the math is straightforward:

$$\text{Yield} = \frac{\$50}{\$1,000} = 5\%$$

> **Result:** In this case, your **Yield = Coupon**.

## Scenario B: Buying at a "Discount"
Imagine interest rates in the general economy rise to 6%. Investors won't pay \$1,000 for your 5% bond anymore because they can get better returns elsewhere. To sell it, you drop the price to **\$900**.

If a new investor buys it for \$900, they still receive the same \$50 annual check. Their yield is:

$$\text{Yield} = \frac{\$50}{\$900} = 5.55\%$$

> **Result:** Even though the coupon remains 5%, the yield is **5.55%** because the investor paid less for the same income.

## Scenario C: Buying at a "Premium"
Now, imagine interest rates drop to 3%. Your 5% bond is now a "hot ticket" item. People are willing to pay more than face value to capture that higher interest. You sell it for **\$1,100**.

The buyer still only gets the $50 annual payment. Their yield is:

$$\text{Yield} = \frac{\$50}{\$1,100} = 4.54\%$$

> **Result:** Here, the yield is **lower** than the coupon because the buyer paid a "premium" to own the bond.

### Summary Table: The Relationship

| Market Price | Price vs. Face Value | Yield vs. Coupon |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$1,000** | At Par | Yield = Coupon |
| **$900** | Discount | Yield > Coupon |
| **$1,100** | Premium | Yield < Coupon |

## Key Components for a bond position

### The Principal (The "Core")

This is the amount of debt you actually own.

- **Quantity**: How many "units" or bonds you bought.

- **Face Value**: Usually \$1,000 per bond. If you own 10 bonds, your total principal position at maturity is $10,000.

- **Cost Basis**: What you actually paid for it. Since bonds trade on a market, your cost basis might be \$950 (at a discount) or \$1,050 (at a premium).

### The Income Stream (The "Flow")

This is the "rent" the issuer pays you for borrowing your money.

- **Accrued Interest**: Since bonds pay interest every six months, if you sell the bond halfway through a cycle, the buyer owes you the interest earned up to that day. This is a vital component of your position's value.

- **Coupon Frequency**: Most bonds pay semi-annually, though some pay monthly or annually.

### The Duration (The "Sensitivity")

This is a technical component that measures **risk**.

- Duration isn't just time; it’s a number (expressed in years) that tells you how much your bond’s price will move if interest rates change.

- Example: If a bond has a duration of 7, and interest rates rise by 1%, your bond’s price will likely drop by about 7%.

### THe Credit Quality (The "Safety")

This defines the likelihood of you getting your money back.

- **Rating**: Usually provided by agencies like Moody’s or S&P (e.g., AAA, BBB, C).

- **Credit Spread**: This is the difference in yield between your bond and a "risk-free" government bond. If this spread widens, it means the market thinks your bond is getting riskier.

## The Time Horizon (The "Timeline")

- **Maturity**: The date the clock stops.

- **Callability**: Some bonds are "callable," meaning the issuer has the right to pay you back early and stop paying interest. If your bond is called, your "position" ends sooner than you planned.

Summary Table: Bond Key Components

| Component | What it tells you |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Market Price** | What the position is worth if you sold it today. |
| **Yield to Maturity (YTM)** | Your total expected return if you hold until the end. |
| **Accrued Interest** | The cash you've "earned" but haven't been paid yet. |
| **Duration** | How much a "rate hike" will hurt your portfolio. |

## Risks of Bond Positions

While bonds are often called "safe" investments, they aren't "risk-free." Even the most stable government bonds carry certain risks that can eat away at your returns or your principal.

### Interest Rate Risk

This is the most common risk. As we discussed with the "teeter-totter" effect, bond prices move in the opposite direction of interest rates.

- **The Danger**: If interest rates rise, the market value of your existing bond will drop. If you need to sell that bond before it matures, you will likely have to sell it for less than you paid.

### Credit Risk (Default Risk)

This is the risk that the issuer—the company or government—simply can’t pay you back.

- **The Danger**: If a company goes bankrupt, they may stop making interest payments and fail to return your principal at maturity.

- **Protection**: Investors use **Credit Ratings** (from agencies like Moody's or S&P) to gauge this. AAA is the "gold standard," while anything below BBB- is considered "High Yield" or "Junk."

### Inflation Risk (Purchasing Power Risk)

Bonds pay a fixed amount of interest. If the cost of living (inflation) rises faster than your bond's interest rate, your "real" return is negative.

- **The Danger**: If your bond pays 3% but inflation is 5%, the money you get back at the end will actually buy fewer groceries or gas than the money you originally lent out.

### Liquidity Risk

Liquidity refers to how easily you can turn an asset into cash without losing value.

- **The Danger**: While US Treasuries are incredibly easy to sell, some corporate or municipal bonds are rarely traded. If you own an obscure bond and need cash fast, you might not find a buyer unless you agree to a massive discount.

### Call Risk

Some bonds are "callable," meaning the issuer can pay you back early.

- **The Danger**: The Danger: Issuers usually do this when interest rates drop. They "call" your high-interest bond and issue new ones at a lower rate. You get your principal back, but you are forced to reinvest that money at a time when market rates are lower.

#### Risk Comparison Table

| Risk Factor | What specifically happens? | Who is most at risk? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Interest Rate** | Bond price drops as rates rise. | Long-term bondholders. |
| **Credit** | Issuer fails to pay. | Corporate/Junk bondholders. |
| **Inflation** | Interest buys less than before. | All fixed-rate bondholders. |
| **Call** | Bond is "cancelled" early by issuer. | Holders of high-coupon bonds. |

## Bond Issuance

Bond issuance is essentially a company or government "crowdsourcing" a massive loan from the public rather than going to a single bank. It allows them to lock in large amounts of capital, often at a lower interest rate or for a longer duration than a traditional bank loan would allow.

#### The Bond Issuance Process

### Bonds vs. Bank Loans: Why Issue Bonds?

| Feature | Bonds (Public Debt) | Bank Loans (Private Debt) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Interest Rates** | **Lower Rates:** Large, stable companies can borrow from the public at lower market rates. | **Higher Rates:** Banks add a profit margin and administrative fees to the base rate. |
| **Collateral** | **Unsecured:** Often "debentures," meaning no specific assets (like buildings) are pledged. | **Secured:** Banks usually require specific collateral to back the loan. |
| **Flexibility** | **Custom Terms:** Issuers can add "callable" features to pay off debt early if rates drop. | **Rigid Terms:** Loans often come with strict restrictive covenants and fixed schedules. |
| **Repayment** | **Bullet Maturity:** Often, the full principal is paid only at the very end. | **Amortization:** Usually requires regular payments of both interest and principal. |

### Key Roles in Issuance

- **The Issuer**: The borrower (Corporation, Municipality, or Federal Government).

- **The Underwriter**: The middleman (Goldman Sachs, JP Morgan, etc.) who guarantees the sale of the bonds.

- **The Trustee**: A third party (usually a bank) that ensures the issuer follows the rules and makes payments on time to the bondholders.

### Why Issue Bonds Instead of Taking a Loan?

- **Lower Rates**: Large, stable companies can often borrow from the public at lower rates than a bank would charge.

- **No Collateral**: Many corporate bonds are "unsecured," meaning the company doesn't have to put up specific assets (like a building) as collateral.

- **Flexibility**: Issuers can customize the terms, such as making the bond "callable" so they can pay it off early if interest rates drop.

## Additional Components of a Bond (Position)

### Factor for Amortizing Bond

In the OpenWealth API (and general financial data standards like ISO 20022), the hasFactor and factor attributes are critical for a specific type of bond called an Amortizing Bond or a Securitized Product (like a Mortgage-Backed Security).

Here is the breakdown of what this attribute means and how it works in your schema:

### What is the "Factor"?
The factor (often called the Pool Factor) represents the remaining principal balance of the bond as a percentage of its original face value.

- Standard Bond: Usually has a factor of 1.0. You get the full principal back at the very end.

- Amortizing Bond: The issuer pays back pieces of the principal over time (not just interest). As they pay you back, the "factor" drops.

### How to use the Math

To find the actual "current value" of the position, you multiply the original nominal amount by this factor.

$$\text{Current Principal} = \text{Original Nominal} \times \text{Factor}$$

#### Example from your schema:

- If your denomination is 1000.0 and the factor is 0.85:
- The bond is currently worth 850.0 in principal.
- The issuer has already paid back 15% of the original loan.

### Why hasFactor is a Boolean

The hasFactor attribute acts as a "flag" for systems to know how to process the data:

- If TRUE: The system must look for a factor value to calculate the correct valuation.

- If TRUE but factor is missing: As your description notes, this is a warning that the data is "factor-dependent" but the bank hasn't updated the specific number yet (common in the first few days of a month).

### Repay Price Factor

In financial data schemas, the repayPriceFactor is a scaling coefficient used to convert a human-readable percentage into a mathematical decimal for calculations.

#### The Core Purpose

Most bonds are quoted in percent of par. For example, a bond "repaying at 100" means it pays back 100% of its face value. However, computers cannot multiply 1000 (Nominal) by 100 (Price) directly to get the value, or the result would be 100,000.

The repayPriceFactor acts as the bridge:

- The Price: 100.0 (Percentage)
- The Factor: 0.01 (The "Decimalizer")
- The Math: $100.0 \times 0.01 = 1.0$ (100%)

#### Why is it useful?

This attribute provides flexibility for different types of financial instruments within the same system:

- **Standard Bonds**: Usually use a factor of 0.01 because they are quoted in percentages (0-100+).

- **Commodities or Units**: Might use a factor of 1.0 if the price is already represented as a direct currency value per unit.

- **Specialized Debt**: Some markets might quote in "basis points" or other units, where the factor would adjust accordingly (e.g., 0.0001).

#### Calculation for Repayment Value:

$$\text{Nominal} \times (\text{Repay Price} \times \text{Repay Price Factor}) = \text{Payout}$$
$$1000.0 \times (100.0 \times 0.01) = 1000.0 \text{ CHF}$$

#### Comparison: Factor vs. Repay Price

It is easy to confuse the factor in the OpenWealth schema with the repayPriceFactor in your previous JSON. They serve different purposes:

| Feature | factor (Pool Factor) | repayPriceFactor |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Purpose** | Shows how much principal is left. | A multiplier to convert percentages to decimals. |
| **Typical Value** | Changes over time (e.g., 1.0 → 0.9 → 0.8). | Usually constant (e.g., 0.01). |
| **Usage** | Used for Mortgages/Asset-Backed Securities. | Used for standard Bond pricing calculations. |

#### Why it matters

In OpenWealth, this factor ensures that your software doesn't accidentally overvalue a position. Without applying the 0.01 factor to a "100" price, a calculation would incorrectly assume the bond is paying back 100 times its face value rather than 100% of its face value.
